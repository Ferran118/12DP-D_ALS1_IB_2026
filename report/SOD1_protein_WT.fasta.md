The c.14C>T substitution is a missense mutation. At the codon level, the nucleotide change alters the fifth codon from GCC to GTC, resulting in an amino acid substitution from Alanine to Valine (p.Ala5Val). 

**Q6: If nucleotides have been inserted or deleted, could the change alter the reading frame (i.e. frameshift)?**

Yes. Adding or losing nucleotides changes the reading frame because ribosomes read mRNA in groups of three (codons). If one nucleotide is added or removed, every group of three after that point gets mixed up. This changes all the following amino acids and usually creates an early stop signal, making a broken protein.

**Q17: Are all nucleotide sequence variants accompanied by a corresponding, equivalent amino-acid sequence variant?**

No, not all nucleotide variants cause a change in the protein sequence. This happens because the genetic code is redundant, meaning several different nucleotide triplets can code for the exact same amino acid. When a nucleotide change does not alter the amino acid, it is called a synonymous (or silent) mutation. Only non-synonymous mutations, such as missense mutations that swap one amino acid for another, result in a corresponding change in the protein.

**Q18: Do the three species appear more similar at the CDS or protein level? Why?**

The sequences are more similar at the protein level than at the CDS level. This is because the genetic code is redundant, allowing multiple distinct codons to specify the same amino acid. As a result, many nucleotide substitutions at the CDS level are synonymous (silent), meaning they do not alter the primary amino acid sequence. This buffers the protein from genetic variation and maintains structural and functional conservation across species despite underlying nucleotide divergence.

**Q20: Is the human disease-associated variant located in a conserved region? Using your project research, what is known about the function of this region of the protein?**

Based on our research and alignment data, the human disease-associated variant is not located in a conserved region, but rather in a variable region (N-terminal, amino acids 1–60), where both orangutans (Pongo abelii) and cattle (Bos taurus) naturally feature a Valine at position 5 compared to the native Alanine in humans. 

Functionally, this region forms part of the SOD1 homodimer contact interface; thus, the substitution with Valine—which is more voluminous and hydrophobic—alters the binding geometry, creates an aberrant hydrophobic block, and compromises zinc ion ($\text{Zn}^{2+}$) coordination. By destabilizing dimerization and native packing, the protein tends to unfold and expose hydrophobic patches that trigger the formation of cytotoxic fibrillar aggregates, which are responsible for motor neuron degeneration in ALS1.
