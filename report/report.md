## Results

### Mutant sequence findings
The wild-type (WT) coding sequence (CDS) matches the human superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene with 100% Query Cover, an E-value of 0.0, and 100.00% Percent Identity. The WT CDS of the protein sequence corresponds to the superoxide dismutase [Cu-Zn], confirming complete identity between the nucleic acid and protein records.

Alignment between the WT and mutant CDS identified a single-nucleotide substitution, c.14C>T. This nucleotide change alters the fifth codon from GCC to GTC. According to the standard genetic code, the wild-type codon GCC translates to Alanine (Ala), whereas the mutated codon GTC translates to Valine (Val). Consequently, this single base change drives a missense mutation at the protein level, resulting in the p.Ala5Val substitution.

### Evolutionary findings
Multiple sequence alignment of the SOD1 coding sequences across Homo sapiens, Pongo abelii, and Bos taurus reveals a highly conserved region between positions 178 and 237. Nonetheless, nucleotide variation is present: the human CDS alignment displays a 3-nucleotide gap at positions 35–37 compared to Pongo abelii, while Bos taurus exhibits additional single-nucleotide substitutions across this segment. At the protein level, SOD1 exhibits higher conservation than at the CDS level. The C-terminal domain (amino acids 121–154) is nearly identical across all three species, showing only three amino acid differences between Pongo abelii and Bos taurus. Similarly, the central domain (amino acids 61–100) is strongly conserved. Conversely, the N-terminal region (amino acids 1–60) harbors the greatest variability, containing several substitutions—such as a Valine at position 5 in Pongo abelii and Bos taurus relative to human Alanine—as well as a 2-amino-acid gap at positions 24–25 unique to Bos taurus.

### Structure and functional impact findings
The c.14C>T (p.Ala5Val) missense mutation replaces a small Alanine residue with a larger, more hydrophobic Valine which favors unwanted protein aggregation. Valine is more voluminous, which can destabilize the protein structure and generates steric distortion (the increasing volume adding the methyl repels the electrons cloud ).
  
Crucially, this amino acid substitution resides within a critical functional region of SOD1: the homodimer interface. The addition of the methyl groups of the Valine interact with a neighboring Valine to generate a hydrophobic block. This alteration severely distorts the geometry of the dimer contact surface, compromising homodimer stability and disrupting proper zinc (Zn2+) coordination.
  
As a result of this increased local hydrophobicity, volume, and structural strain, the SOD1 mutant exhibits a high propensity to unfold. Once unfolded or destabilized, the exposed hydrophobic surfaces drive the formation of cytotoxic protein aggregates, a hallmark of ALS1 pathogenesis.

**Structure:**

At the coding sequence level, this nucleotide substitution alters the fifth codon from "GCC" to "GTC", resulting in the substitution of an Alanine  with a Valine at position 5 of the 154-amino-acid polypeptide chain, **p.Ala5Val**(1),(2).

At the secondary structure level, the added side-chain bulk alters local backbone dynamics by decreasing the propensity for alpha helix formation and favoring a rigid folding such as beta sheets. Quantitative 3D structural superposition reveals a local backbone displacement of 0.379 Å at variant position 5 and an overall global Carbon alpha RMSD of 0.667 Å between wild-type and mutant structures. Although this physical alteration is minimal, its functional impact is dictated by the physicochemical properties mentioned previously, which ultimately destabilize and unfold the protein.
In the Wild-type state of SOD1 it contains a Copper ion ($\\text{Cu}^{2+}$) and a strctural Zinc ion ($\\text{Zn}^{2+}$). Residue 5 lies within the variable N-terminal domain (residues 1–60), which forms an essential part of the homodimer contact interface. This structural stress destabilizes the interface between the two subunits, causing the homodimer to separate into individual monomers. Consequently, the protein loses its structural zinc ion $\\text{Zn}^{2+}$, which its function consists in keep the homodimer structure and leads to unfold the protein(1),(2).

**Function:**
The primary biological role of wild-type SOD1 is to function as an antioxidant enzyme, catalyzing the dismutation of toxic superoxide radicals $\\text{O}\_2^{\\bullet -}$ produced during metabolic respiration into hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) and molecular oxygen ($\text{O}_2$). Also, catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) to sulfate, playing an important role in detoxifying H2S and limiting the accumulation of reactive sulfur species (RSS) such as persulfides and polysulfides(2). The p.Ala5Val mutation reduces overall protein stability and impairs catalytic enzymatic activity. However, the principal disease mechanism in ALS1 is not a simple loss of function, but rather a toxic gain of function. When mutant SOD1 loses its structural zinc ion or dissociates into monomers, it partially unfolds and exposes both its internal hydrophobic core and the newly introduced Valine side chain. To minimize contact with the aqueous cellular environment, these exposed hydrophobic surfaces drive mutant SOD1 molecules to abnormally self-assemble into cytotoxic, amiloid-like fibrillar aggregates, which ultimately trigger motor neuron degeneration in ALS1(2),(3).



**Impact on Patient Function** - *Pathophysiology & Clinical Phenotype*

The primary physiological mechanism linking molecular SOD1 defects to patient dysfunction is the accumulation of misfolded Superoxide Dismutase 1 fibrillar aggregates within the neuronal cytoplasm. Motor neurons possess exceptionally long axons and high metabolic demands, making them uniquely sensitive to aggregate-induced toxicity. The accumulation of these aggregates clogs cellular machinery, driving pathogenesis through three primary mechanisms: 

The disruption of axonal transport happens when insoluble aggregates impede both anterograde and retrograde transport, impairing the delivery of essential proteins, organelles, and nutrients along the long axons of motor neuron. 

Mithocondrial disfunction happens when aggregates associate with the mitochondrial membrane, causing cellular energy failure and exacerbating intracellular oxidative stress. 

Endoplasmic reticulum stress, ER being the responsible part of the cell for protein folding, stress occurs when too many misfolded proteins build up, overloading the cell's system for fixing them and ultimately triggering a programmed cell death.

*Clinical Phenotype and Symptom Progression*

The progressive apoptosis of upper motor neurons (located in the motor cortex) and lower motor neurons (located in the brainstem and spinal cord) breaks communication channels between the central nervous system and skeletal muscles.

As motor unit degeneration advances, patients experience a characteristically progressive clinical phenotype with early motor symptoms such as muscle weakness, muscle atrophy (amyotrophy), fasciculations (involuntary muscle twitching caused by spontaneous nerve firing), and spasticity in the limbs, severely affecting voluntary tasks such as walking, gripping, and fine motor movement. 

Degeneration and damage of the motor neurons in the brainstem lead to dysarthria (difficulty speaking and articulating) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), these impairments carry critical medical risks for the patient, predisposing them to severe malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia.

At the end stage, progressive weakness spreads to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, culminating in severe hypoventilation, fatal respiratory paralysis and failure.

*Genotype-Phenotype Correlation and Prognosis*

The clinical severity, age of onset (patient's age when those first symptoms become noticeable), and rate of disease progression vary significantly depending on the specific SOD1 point mutation present in the patient:

·A4V Mutation —> This mutation is associated with a highly aggressive, rapidly progressive clinical phenotype, resulting in severe early quadriparesis and a mean survival time of approximately 1.2 years post-onset (onset: when the first symptoms of the disease appear).
·Homozygous D90A Mutation —> This mutation displays an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern associated with a much slower, indolent disease progression, with patient survival often exceeding 10 years.

Despite variations in survival rates, the ultimate functional outcome for all affected patients is severe motor disability followed by terminal respiratory muscle failure.

## Conclusions

Ultimately, this project shows how a single tiny mistake in the SuperoxideDismutase 1 gene can cause the entire motor system to fail. It proves that motor neurons are specially sensitive when damaged proteins clump together. By following the disease from a microscopic protein mistake to a patient's physical weakness, we can clearly see how biology controls the illness. 
The fact that some patients decline rapidly while others survive for over a decade, only because of a different genetic mutation, shows how complex ALS truly is. Understanding exactly how and where the cells break down is not just research, since finding these exact points of failure is the first and most important step towards creating a targeted treatment for patients.
