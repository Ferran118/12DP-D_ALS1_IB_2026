## Background
## **Analysis of ALS1 across four specific biological levels**

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the death of motor neurons in the brain, brainstem, and spinal cord, resulting in fatal paralysis. ALS usually begins with asymmetric involvement of the muscles in middle adult life. Approximately 10% of ALS cases are familial. It is genetically heterogeneous, meaning that the disease phenotype is not linked to a single genetic locus, but can be triggered by pathogenic mutations across multiple distinct genes (e.g., SOD1, C9orf72, FUS, and TARDBP). Consequently, the precise genetic cause can vary significantly between patients.

Today we're going to break down the cause of the ALS1 disease on four biological levels: gene, protein, structure, and function.

## Genetic Level Causation

### The SOD1 Gene and Locus. (Genetic heterogeneity of ALS)

Our study focuses on the SOD1 (Superoxide Dismutase 1) gene, located at chromosome 21 at locus 21q22.11.

SOD1 was the first gene identified as a causative agent in ALS.

Pathogenic variants in SOD1 account for approximately 12–20% of familial ALS (fALS) cases, and 1–2% of sporadic ALS (sALS) cases worldwide.

* **Mutation Profile:** Scientists have found 150 distinct mutations in the SOD1 gene that have been linked to ALS. The vast majority are missense mutations, single nucleotide substitution that leads to an aminoacid replacement in the resulting polypeptide chain. A classic example is the A5V mutation, being a substitution of alanine for valine at position 5 - which is associated with a rapidly progressive disease phenotype.
* **Inheritance can be:**

  * **Autosomal Dominant (AD):** The primary mode of inheritance for SOD1-linked ALS. A single inherited copy of the mutated allele from one parent is sufficient to trigger disease pathogenesis (e.g., the A5V variant).
  * **Autosomal Recessive (AR):** Exceptionally, certain SOD1 variants display recessive inheritance, requiring two copies of the mutated allele (one from each parent) for disease expression (e.g., homozygous D90A mutation).

---

## Protein Level: The Cu/Zn-SOD Enzyme

The SOD1 gene provides the instructions for creating the copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/Zn-SOD) enzyme.

- **Composition:** The mature, healthy protein consists of 154 amino acids.
- The SOD1 protein, whether it is normal or mutated (wild-form or ALS1), if intramolecular disulfide bond or of bound zinc ions are absent, this protein has a tendency to form fibrillar aggregates/clumps. These aggregates may have cytotoxic effects - in other words - deadly for cells. Without its zinc and structural clips, the protein falls apart and destroys the motor neurons, so Zinc binding promotes dimerization and stabilizes the native form.
- **Quaternary State:** It naturally operates as a homodimer, meaning two identical SOD1 protein subunits must bind together to function properly. (In a non-covalent bond)
- **Cofactors:** Each SOD1 subunit requires the binding of two essential metal ions. A copper ion ($\text{Cu}^{2+}$) is critical for the enzyme's catalytic activity - while a zinc ion ($\text{Zn}^{2+}$) is required to promote dimerization and stabilize the protein's native three-dimensional folding.

### Normal Physiological Protein Function

Physiologically, the protein functions as a vital antioxidant metalloenzyme, destroying the toxic superoxide radicals that are naturally produced within cells, therefore protecting biological systems from oxidative stress.

Furthermore, SOD1 catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) to sulfate. This process plays an important role for detoxifying $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and limiting the cellular accumulation of reactive sulfur species (RSS), such as persulfides and polysulfides.

### Pathological Mechanism (Fibrillar Aggregation)

Since the structural integrity is heavily dependent on its bound zinc ions and an internal structural clip,  the absence of either this disulfide bond or the bound zinc, both wild-type and ALS1-associated mutant SOD1 proteins become structurally unstable. This instability causes the protein to misfold, giving it a tendency to form fibrillar aggregates. These insoluble clumps exert severe cytotoxic effects, driving motor neuron degeneration.

---

## Structural Level: Destabilization and Aggregation

At the structural level, mutant SOD1 proteins undergo severe conformational changes that lead directly to disease pathology.

- **Normal Structure:** Healthy SOD1 folds into a highly stable, eight-stranded anti-parallel beta-barrel.
- **Mutant Instability:** ALS-associated genetic mutations disrupt the protein's native folding. This severe structural instability ultimately triggers dimer dissociation, forcing the stable complex to break apart into vulnerable monomers.
- **Misfolding and Aggregation:** The dissociation of the dimer and the unfolding of the protein expose hydrophobic regions that are normally safely buried within the native structure. These destabilized, misfolded SOD1 monomers inappropriately interact with one another, initiating a cascade where they assemble into insoluble oligomers and large fibrillar aggregates. These conformational changes and the resulting clumps lead directly to disease pathology.

---

## Functional Level: Toxic Gain-of-Function

The functional breakdown of SOD1 affects specifically motor neurons.

- **Normal Function:** Healthy SOD1 acts as an essential cellular antioxidant. It scavenges highly reactive superoxide radicals ($\text{O}_2^-$)—toxic byproducts of mitochondrial energy production—and neutralizes them into ordinary molecular oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) and hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$).
- **The ALS1 Mechanism:** While some loss of this antioxidant function occurs, ALS1 is driven primarily by a toxic gain-of-function. The disease is not caused by the absence of normal SOD1, but by the new, harmful properties of the mutated version.
- **Motor Neuron Death:** The structurally misfolded SOD1 aggregates physically accumulate in the cytoplasm of motor neurons. These aggregates choke the cell's internal machinery: they disrupt axonal transport, impair mitochondrial function, and induce severe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. This overwhelming cellular toxicity eventually triggers apoptosis (programmed cell death), severing the communication network between the brain and muscles and resulting in fatal paralysis.

## Results

### Mutant sequence findings
The wild-type (WT) coding sequence (CDS) matches the human superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene with 100% Query Cover, an E-value of 0.0, and 100.00% Percent Identity. The WT CDS of the protein sequence corresponds to the superoxide dismutase [Cu-Zn], confirming complete identity between the nucleic acid and protein records.

Alignment between the WT and mutant CDS identified a single-nucleotide substitution, c.14C>T. This nucleotide change alters the fifth codon from GCC to GTC. According to the standard genetic code, the wild-type codon GCC translates to Alanine (Ala), whereas the mutated codon GTC translates to Valine (Val). Consequently, this single base change drives a missense mutation at the protein level, resulting in the p.Ala5Val substitution.

### Evolutionary findings
Multiple sequence alignment of the SOD1 coding sequences across Homo sapiens, Pongo abelii, and Bos taurus reveals a highly conserved region between positions 178 and 237. Nonetheless, nucleotide variation is present: the human CDS alignment displays a 3-nucleotide gap at positions 35–37 compared to Pongo abelii, while Bos taurus exhibits additional single-nucleotide substitutions across this segment. At the protein level, SOD1 exhibits higher conservation than at the CDS level. The C-terminal domain (amino acids 121–154) is nearly identical across all three species, showing only three amino acid differences between Pongo abelii and Bos taurus. Similarly, the central domain (amino acids 61–100) is strongly conserved. Conversely, the N-terminal region (amino acids 1–60) harbors the greatest variability, containing several substitutions—such as a Valine at position 5 in Pongo abelii and Bos taurus relative to human Alanine—as well as a 2-amino-acid gap at positions 24–25 unique to Bos taurus.

### Structure and functional impact findings
The c.14C>T (p.Ala5Val) missense mutation replaces a small Alanine residue with a larger, more hydrophobic Valine which favors unwanted protein aggregation. Valine is more voluminous, which can destabilize the protein structure and generates steric distortion (the increasing volume adding the methyl repels the electrons cloud ).
  
Crucially, this amino acid substitution resides within a critical functional region of SOD1: the homodimer interface. The addition of the methyl groups of the Valine interact with a neighboring Valine to generate a hydrophobic block. This alteration severely distorts the geometry of the dimer contact surface, compromising homodimer stability and disrupting proper zinc (Zn2+) coordination.
  
As a result of this increased local hydrophobicity, volume, and structural strain, the SOD1 mutant exhibits a high propensity to unfold. Once unfolded or destabilized, the exposed hydrophobic surfaces drive the formation of cytotoxic protein aggregates, a hallmark of ALS1 pathogenesis.

**Function:**
The primary biological role of wild-type SOD1 is to function as an antioxidant enzyme, catalyzing the dismutation of toxic superoxide radicals $\\text{O}\_2^{\\bullet -}$ produced during metabolic respiration into hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) and molecular oxygen ($\text{O}_2$). Also, catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) to sulfate, playing an important role in detoxifying H2S and limiting the accumulation of reactive sulfur species (RSS) such as persulfides and polysulfides(2). The p.Ala5Val mutation reduces overall protein stability and impairs catalytic enzymatic activity. However, the principal disease mechanism in ALS1 is not a simple loss of function, but rather a toxic gain of function. When mutant SOD1 loses its structural zinc ion or dissociates into monomers, it partially unfolds and exposes both its internal hydrophobic core and the newly introduced Valine side chain. To minimize contact with the aqueous cellular environment, these exposed hydrophobic surfaces drive mutant SOD1 molecules to abnormally self-assemble into cytotoxic, amiloid-like fibrillar aggregates, which ultimately trigger motor neuron degeneration in ALS1(2),(3).



**Impact on Patient Function** - *Pathophysiology & Clinical Phenotype*

The primary physiological mechanism linking molecular SOD1 defects to patient dysfunction is the accumulation of misfolded Superoxide Dismutase 1 fibrillar aggregates within the neuronal cytoplasm. Motor neurons possess exceptionally long axons and high metabolic demands, making them uniquely sensitive to aggregate-induced toxicity. The accumulation of these aggregates clogs cellular machinery, driving pathogenesis through three primary mechanisms: 

The disruption of axonal transport happens when insoluble aggregates impede both anterograde and retrograde transport, impairing the delivery of essential proteins, organelles, and nutrients along the long axons of motor neuron. 

Mithocondrial disfunction happens when aggregates associate with the mitochondrial membrane, causing cellular energy failure and exacerbating intracellular oxidative stress. 

Endoplasmic reticulum stress, ER being the responsible part of the cell for protein folding, stress occurs when too many misfolded proteins build up, overloading the cell's system for fixing them and ultimately triggering a programmed cell death.

*Clinical Phenotype and Symptom Progression*

The progressive apoptosis of upper motor neurons (located in the motor cortex) and lower motor neurons (located in the brainstem and spinal cord) breaks communication channels between the central nervous system and skeletal muscles.

As motor unit degeneration advances, patients experience a characteristically progressive clinical phenotype with early motor symptoms such as muscle weakness, muscle atrophy (amyotrophy), fasciculations (involuntary muscle twitching caused by spontaneous nerve firing), and spasticity in the limbs, severely affecting voluntary tasks such as walking, gripping, and fine motor movement. 

Degeneration and damage of the motor neurons in the brainstem lead to dysarthria (difficulty speaking and articulating) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), these impairments carry critical medical risks for the patient, predisposing them to severe malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia.

At the end stage, progressive weakness spreads to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, culminating in severe hypoventilation, fatal respiratory paralysis and failure.

*Genotype-Phenotype Correlation and Prognosis*

The clinical severity, age of onset (patient's age when those first symptoms become noticeable), and rate of disease progression vary significantly depending on the specific SOD1 point mutation present in the patient:

·A4V Mutation —> This mutation is associated with a highly aggressive, rapidly progressive clinical phenotype, resulting in severe early quadriparesis and a mean survival time of approximately 1.2 years post-onset (onset: when the first symptoms of the disease appear).
·Homozygous D90A Mutation —> This mutation displays an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern associated with a much slower, indolent disease progression, with patient survival often exceeding 10 years.

Despite variations in survival rates, the ultimate functional outcome for all affected patients is severe motor disability followed by terminal respiratory muscle failure.


## Discussion

### Sequence Alignment and Physicochemical Alterations
Our molecular analysis of the *SOD1* gene provides direct insight into how a single genomic disruption initiates the complex cascade driving Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis 1 (ALS1). Pairwise alignment confirmed that the wild-type coding sequence matches human *SOD1* with 100% query cover and complete identity. The c.14C>T single-nucleotide substitution in exon 1 alters the fifth codon from GCC to GTC, translating into the missense substitution p.Ala5Val within the 154-amino-acid protein. From a physicochemical standpoint, replacing a small Alanine residue with a larger, more hydrophobic Valine introduces significant spatial and energetic tension. Valine’s bulky, branched side chain increases molecular volume and generates steric strain, as the electron clouds of its additional methyl groups repel surrounding atomic structures. At the secondary structure level, this added bulk disrupts backbone dynamics, decreasing the local propensity for α-helix formation and favoring a rigid β-sheet conformation.

### Structural Distortion and Dimer Disruption
Quantitative 3D superposition reveals a local backbone displacement of 0.379 Å at variant position 4/5 and an overall Cα Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) of 0.667 Å relative to wild-type structures (PDB ID: 2C9V). Although this spatial shift is minor, its functional impact is severe because residue 4/5 lies within the N-terminal domain (residues 1–60), an essential component of the non-covalent homodimer contact interface. The newly introduced methyl groups of Valine interact with neighboring hydrophobic residues to form a bulky hydrophobic block, distorting interface geometry and destabilizing subunit binding. This structural stress causes the homodimer to separate into individual monomers, leading to the loss of the structural zinc ion (Zn²⁺) responsible for maintaining native tertiary folding. Deprived of its zinc anchor, mutant SOD1 unfolds, exposing its internal hydrophobic core and the Valine side chain to the cytosolic environment.

### Evolutionary Analysis and Structural Constraints
Multiple sequence alignment across *Homo sapiens*, *Pongo abelii*, and *Bos taurus* demonstrates that amino acid conservation is significantly higher than CDS conservation due to genetic code degeneracy. Synonymous substitutions buffer the protein against DNA sequence divergence, preserving structural constraints over evolutionary time. Ultra-conserved residues across mammalian orthologs form catalytic active sites, coordinate metal cofactors (Cu²⁺) and (Zn²⁺), establish the intramolecular disulfide bridge (Cys57–Cys146), or stabilize the core eight-stranded anti-parallel β-barrel fold. Mutations at these ultra-conserved positions impair biological function and are purged by strong purifying selection.

Interestingly, the human disease variant (p.Ala4Val) does not reside within an ultra-conserved catalytic loop, but in the structurally variable N-terminal region where *Pongo abelii* and *Bos taurus* naturally feature Valine at position 5. Functionally, however, this region forms part of the human homodimer contact interface. Substituting Alanine for Valine alters human binding geometry, creates an unnatural hydrophobic patch, and impairs (Zn²⁺) coordination. That Valine-5 is tolerated in orangutans and cattle shows it is not inherently toxic in all structural contexts; its pathogenicity in humans stems from breaking co-evolved hydrophobic contact networks unique to human homodimer packing.

### Molecular Pathophysiology and Toxic Gain-of-Function
Under normal physiological conditions, wild-type Cu/Zn-SOD acts as an essential antioxidant, dismutating toxic superoxide radicals (O₂•-) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and oxygen (O₂), while also oxidizing hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) to sulfate to prevent reactive sulfur species (RSS) accumulation (Suthar & Lee, 2023). Although p.Ala5Val reduces protein stability and catalytic activity, ALS1 pathogenesis is primarily driven by a toxic gain-of-function (Redler & Dokholyan, 2012). Exposed hydrophobic surfaces on zinc-deficient, unfolded monomers drive aberrant self-assembly into cytotoxic, amyloid-like fibrillar aggregates (Redler & Dokholyan, 2012; Suthar & Lee, 2023). Motor neurons are uniquely sensitive to aggregate toxicity due to their extreme axonal lengths (up to 1 meter) and high metabolic demands. Cytosolic aggregate accumulation clogs cellular machinery via three core mechanisms:

1. Axonal Transport Disruption: Impairing anterograde and retrograde motor-protein transport along extended axons.
2. Mitochondrial Dysfunction: Binding to outer mitochondrial membranes, inducing ATP failure and oxidative stress.
3. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) Stress: Overwhelming protein-folding capacity and triggering apoptotic pathways.

### Clinical Phenotype Mapping and Genotype-Phenotype Correlations
Progressive apoptosis of upper motor neurons (motor cortex) and lower motor neurons (brainstem/spinal cord) severs signaling channels to skeletal target muscles. Patients experience progressive early motor symptoms including muscle weakness, amyotrophy, fasciculations, and limb spasticity (Gagliardi et al., 2023). Bulbar motor neuron degeneration causes dysarthria and dysphagia, predisposing patients to severe malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia. Terminal disease spreads to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, culminating in severe hypoventilation and fatal respiratory failure.

Clinical severity and progression rate vary by mutation: the p.Ala4Val variant correlates with an aggressive phenotype marked by rapid quadriparesis and a mean survival of ~1.2 years post-onset, whereas the homozygous p.Asp90Ala variant displays an autosomal recessive pattern with an indolent course where survival often exceeds 10 years (Gagliardi et al., 2023). Across all variants, the uniform terminal outcome remains severe motor disability followed by fatal respiratory failure.

### Limitations and Future Directions
Study limitations include focusing primarily on p.Ala4Val and D90A, restricting evolutionary sampling to three mammals, relying on static PDB coordinates (2C9V) without explicit-solvent molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and lacking in vitro biophysical validation. Future research should expand sequence alignments further down the phylogenetic tree (non-mammalian vertebrates and invertebrates) to trace ancestral core evolution, execute long-timescale MD simulations of monomer unfolding, and perform virtual screening of small-molecule chaperones to stabilize the N-terminal homodimer interface.

## Conclusions

Ultimately, this project shows how a single tiny mistake in the SuperoxideDismutase 1 gene can cause the entire motor system to fail. It proves that motor neurons are specially sensitive when damaged proteins clump together. By following the disease from a microscopic protein mistake to a patient's physical weakness, we can clearly see how biology controls the illness. 
The fact that some patients decline rapidly while others survive for over a decade, only because of a different genetic mutation, shows how complex ALS truly is. Understanding exactly how and where the cells break down is not just research, since finding these exact points of failure is the first and most important step towards creating a targeted treatment for patients.


## Methods

### Data sources
To investigate the molecular and systemic basis of Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis 1 (ALS1), we retrieved the complete wild-type coding sequence (CDS) and corresponding amino acid sequence of human superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) from the NCBI Nucleotide and Gene databases (RefSeq NM_000454.5, Gene ID 6647). Genomic coordinates at locus 21q22.11 and exon-intron architecture were mapped using NCBI RefSeq. Genetic, phenotypic, and clinical data regarding ALS1 and primary SOD1 variants, specifically p.Ala4Val (A4V) and p.Asp90Ala (D90A), were obtained from the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM #105400) and UniProtKB databases (UniProt ID: P00441, Disease ID: DI-00108). Enzymatic roles, metal cofactor bindings (Cu²⁺) and (Zn²⁺), and literature-curated cellular mechanisms were extracted from PubMed (PMID: 18948262, PMID: 24140062, PMID: 36630448). For cross-species conservation analysis, orthologous CDS and protein records for Pongo abelii (Gene ID: 100170281, RefSeq NM_001131969.1) and Bos taurus (Gene ID: 281495) were collected from NCBI Gene. Finally, three-dimensional atomic crystallographic coordinates for the human wild-type Cu/Zn-SOD homodimer were acquired from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 2C9V).

### Implementation
Pairwise nucleotide sequence alignments between the wild-type SOD1 CDS and mutant query sequences were executed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool for nucleotides (BLASTn) under default parameters to verify single-nucleotide point substitutions, specifically confirming the c.14C>T mutation in exon 1 driving the p.Ala4Val (A4V) substitution. The nucleotide sequence was translated in silico using the standard genetic code to evaluate open reading frame continuity and amino acid replacements. For evolutionary comparative analysis, multiple sequence alignments (MSA) of both CDS and amino acid sequences across Homo sapiens, Pongo abelii, and Bos taurus were computed using Clustal Omega to map structural and functional domains across the mature 154-amino-acid polypeptide. These mapped domains included the catalytic copper (Cu²⁺) site, structural zinc (Zn²⁺) site, conserved intramolecular disulfide bridge (Cys57–Cys146), and the eight-stranded anti-parallel β-barrel fold. Additionally, the target p.Ala4Val substitution was modeled on the three-dimensional atomic crystallographic structure (PDB ID: 2C9V) to analyze side-chain volume expansion, spatial packing alterations, and local backbone strain at the non-covalent homodimer contact interface.

To model downstream disease mechanics, we integrated a standardized pathophysiological mapping framework. Cytotoxicity was modeled through cytosolic misfolded SOD1 fibrillar aggregate accumulation in motor neurons, parameterized across three primary subcellular failure pathways:

1. Axonal Transport Disruption: Impediment of both anterograde and retrograde motor transport across extended axons.
2. Mitochondrial Dysfunction: Aggregate binding to outer mitochondrial membranes, inducing cellular energy failure and oxidative stress.
3. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER) Stress: Accumulation of misfolded proteins exceeding ER refolding capacity, initiating apoptotic pathways.

At the systemic level, progressive loss of upper (motor cortex) and lower (brainstem/spinal cord) motor neurons was mapped to clinical phenotypes, tracking early symptoms (muscle weakness, amyotrophy, fasciculations, spasticity), bulbar impairments (dysarthria, dysphagia leading to malnutrition and aspiration pneumonia risk), and terminal respiratory failure driven by diaphragm and intercostal muscle paralysis. Furthermore, genotype-phenotype correlations were parameterized using clinical prognosis metrics: the p.Ala4Val (A4V) mutation was mapped to a rapidly progressive quadriparesis phenotype with a mean post-onset survival of ~1.2 years, whereas the homozygous p.Asp90Ala (D90A) mutation was mapped under an autosomal recessive model with an indolent course and survival exceeding 10 years.

### Numbering convention
Sequence alignment statistics generated via BLASTn were evaluated based on Query Cover percentage, Percent Identity, and Expectation values (*E*-value), with statistical significance established at an *E*-value cutoff of *0.0*. Evolutionary amino acid conservation and the biological substitution tolerance of the p.Ala4Val (A4V) mutation across mammalian orthologs were scored using the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix, analyzing log-odds ratios to categorize conservative versus non-conservative replacements. Quantitative 3D structural superposition between wild-type and mutant crystallographic structures (PDB ID: 2C9V) was evaluated using Cα Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) in angstroms (Å) and local backbone displacement metrics to measure spatial packing strain and energetic disruption at the homodimer contact interface.

To ensure strict clarity across genetic and structural databases, variant nomenclature follows the Human Genome Variation Society (HGVS) standard relative to the mature 154-amino-acid SOD1 polypeptide. Under mature protein numbering (excluding the N-terminal initiator Methionine post-translationally cleaved in active human Cu/Zn-SOD), the alanine-to-valine substitution at codon 14 (c.14C>T) is designated as p.Ala4Val (A4V). This corresponds precisely to position 5 (p.Ala5Val) when referencing the full unprocessed precursor translation (RefSeq NP_000445.1) or full-length structural coordinates.
