# DISCUSSION
## Sequence Alignment and Physicochemical Alterations
Our molecular analysis of the *SOD1* gene provides direct insight into how a single genomic disruption initiates the complex cascade driving Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis 1 (ALS1). Pairwise alignment confirmed that the wild-type coding sequence matches human *SOD1* with 100% query cover and complete identity. The c.14C>T single-nucleotide substitution in exon 1 alters the fifth codon from GCC to GTC, translating into the missense substitution p.Ala5Val within the 154-amino-acid protein. From a physicochemical standpoint, replacing a small Alanine residue with a larger, more hydrophobic Valine introduces significant spatial and energetic tension. Valine’s bulky, branched side chain increases molecular volume and generates steric strain, as the electron clouds of its additional methyl groups repel surrounding atomic structures. At the secondary structure level, this added bulk disrupts backbone dynamics, decreasing the local propensity for α-helix formation and favoring a rigid β-sheet conformation.

## Structural Distortion and Dimer Disruption
Quantitative 3D structural superposition reveals a local backbone displacement of 0.379 Å at position 5 and a global Cα Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD) of 0.667 Å between wild-type and mutant structures. While this spatial shift appears minor, its functional repercussions are severe because residue 5 lies within the variable N-terminal region (residues 1–60), an essential component of the non-covalent homodimer contact interface. The newly introduced methyl groups of Valine interact with neighboring Valine residues to form a bulky hydrophobic block, distorting the dimer interface geometry and destabilizing subunit binding. This structural stress drives the functional homodimer to separate into individual monomers. Consequently, the protein loses its structural zinc ion (Zn²⁺), which is normally responsible for maintaining tertiary folding and dimer stability. Deprived of its zinc anchor, the mutant SOD1 protein unfolds, exposing both its internal hydrophobic core and the Valine side chain to the cytosolic environment.

## Evolutionary Analysis and Sequence Constraints
Sequence conservation across mammalian species is significantly higher at the protein level than at the coding sequence (CDS) level. This pattern is driven by the degeneracy of the genetic code, which allows distinct nucleotide codons to specify the exact same amino acid. Consequently, many base substitutions within the CDS represent synonymous (silent) mutations, effectively buffering the protein against DNA sequence divergence and preserving SOD1 structural and functional constraints over evolutionary time.

Highly conserved residues typically execute critical biological roles: they form catalytic active sites, coordinate essential metal cofactors (Cu²⁺) and (Zn²⁺), form intramolecular disulfide bonds, or stabilize the core eight-stranded anti-parallel β-barrel fold. Because mutations at these ultra-conserved positions disrupt structural stability or enzymatic activity, they impair biological function and are purged by strong purifying (negative) selection.

Interestingly, alignment data indicate that the human disease-causing variant (p.Ala5Val) does not reside within one of these ultra-conserved domains. Instead, it is located in a structurally variable region (the N-terminal domain, amino acids 1–60), where both *Pongo abelii* and *Bos taurus* naturally feature a Valine at position 5 relative to the native human Alanine. Functionally, however, this region forms part of the SOD1 homodimer contact interface. Substituting the native Alanine for Valine, a bulkier and more hydrophobic residue, alters the binding geometry, creates an unnatural hydrophobic patch, and impairs proper (Zn²⁺) coordination. By destabilizing the dimer interface and disrupting native tertiary packing, the mutant protein undergoes misfolding and exposes hydrophobic surfaces, driving the formation of cytotoxic fibrillar aggregates characteristic of ALS1 pathology.

## Toxic Gain-of-Function and Clinical Phenotype
Under normal physiological conditions, wild-type SOD1 acts as an essential antioxidant, scavenging toxic superoxide radicals (O₂•-) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and oxygen (O₂), while also oxidizing hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) to sulfate to prevent reactive sulfur species accumulation. Although the p.Ala5Val mutation impairs this catalytic activity, the core driver of ALS1 is not a simple loss of function, but a toxic gain-of-function. To avoid contact with the aqueous cellular environment, the exposed hydrophobic surfaces of destabilized monomers drive the protein to aberrantly self-assemble into cytotoxic, amyloid-like fibrillar aggregates. As these toxic clumps build up within upper and lower motor neurons, they induce severe oxidative stress, ruin mitochondrial energetics, and trigger cell death pathways. Ultimately, this microscopic molecular failure manifests in the patient as progressive muscle weakness, severe atrophy, and fatal paralysis.
