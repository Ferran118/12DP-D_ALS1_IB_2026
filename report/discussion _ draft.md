# DISCUSSION
## Evolutionary analysis
Sequence conservation across mammalian species is significantly higher at the protein level than at the coding sequence (CDS) level. This pattern is driven by the degeneracy of the genetic code, which allows distinct nucleotide codons to specify the exact same amino acid. Consequently, many base substitutions within the CDS represent synonymous (silent) mutations, effectively buffering the protein against DNA sequence divergence and preserving SOD1 structural and functional constraints over evolutionary time.

Highly conserved residues typically execute critical biological roles: they form catalytic active sites, coordinate essential metal cofactors (Cu2+ and Zn2+), form intramolecular disulfide bonds, or stabilize the core eight-stranded anti-parallel -barrel fold. Because mutations at these ultra-conserved positions disrupt structural stability or enzymatic activity, they impair biological function and are purged by strong purifying (negative) selection.

Interestingly, alignment data indicate that the human disease-causing variant (p.Ala5Val) does not reside within one of these ultra-conserved domains. Instead, it is located in a structurally variable region (the N-terminal domain, amino acids 1–60), where both Pongo abelii and Bos taurus naturally feature a Valine at position 5 relative to the native human Alanine. Functionally, however, this region forms part of the SOD1 homodimer contact interface. Substituting the native Alanine for Valine—a bulkier and more hydrophobic residue—alters the binding geometry, creates an unnatural hydrophobic patch, and impairs proper Zn2+ coordination. By destabilizing the dimer interface and disrupting native tertiary packing, the mutant protein undergoes misfolding and exposes hydrophobic surfaces, driving the formation of cytotoxic fibrillar aggregates characteristic of ALS1 pathology.
