# Luz: Disease Specialist | analysis of ALS1 across four specific biological levels

**Description:** Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the death of motor neurons in the brain, brainstem, and spinal cord, resulting in fatal paralysis. ALS usually begins with asymmetric involvement of the muscles in middle adult life. Approximately 10% of ALS cases are familial.

Today we're going to break down the cause of the ALS1 disease on four biological levels: gene, protein, structure, and function.

## 1. Genetic Level: The *SOD1* Gene
Genetic Heterogeneity of Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis:
ALS is a genetically heterogeneous disorder, with several causative genes and mapped loci. What genetic heterogeneity means is that a disease isn't caused by just one single genetic mistake, but can be triggered by many different ones, the patient's DNA mutaton can vary from person to person. We are going to be focusing on the SOD1 gene, which is one of the involved mutated genes that cause the disease.
The ***SOD1* (Superoxide Dismutase 1) gene** is found on chromosome 21 (21q22.11). 
* **Mutation Profile:** Over 150 distinct mutations in the *SOD1* gene have been linked to ALS. The vast majority are missense mutations (a single nucleotide change resulting in a different amino acid), such as the well-studied A4V (alanine to valine at position 4) mutation.
* **Inheritance:** Familial ALS is caused by mutations in the SOD1 gene usually causes autosomal dominant disease, but can also cause autosomal recessive ALS. Meaning a single inherited copy of the mutated gene is sufficient to trigger the disease.

## 2. Protein Level: The Cu/Zn-SOD Enzyme
The *SOD1* gene provides the blueprint for creating the **copper/zinc superoxide dismutase (Cu/Zn-SOD)** enzyme.
* **Composition:** The mature, healthy protein consists of 154 amino acids.
* **trial** The SOD1 protein, whether it is normal or mutated (wild-form or ALS1), if intramolecular disulfide bond or of bound zinc ions are absent, this protein has a tendency to form fibrillar aggregates/clumps. These aggregates may have cytotoxic effects, in other words, deadly for cells. Without its zinc and structural clips, the protein falls apart and destroys the motor neurons, so Zinc binding promotes dimerization and stabilizes the native form.
* **Quaternary State:** It naturally operates as a homodimer, meaning two identical SOD1 protein subunits must bind together to function properly.
* **Cofactors:** Each protein subunit requires one copper ion (critical for the enzyme's catalytic activity) and one zinc ion (essential for the protein's overall folding and stability).
* **FUNCTION** Destroys radicals which are normally produced within the cells and which are toxic to biological systems (PubMed:18948262, PubMed:24140062).
Catalyzes the oxidation of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) to sulfate, playing an important role in detoxifying H2S and limiting the accumulation of reactive sulfur species (RSS) such as persulfides and polysulfides (PubMed:36630448).

## 3. Structural Level: Destabilization and Aggregation
At the structural level, mutant SOD1 proteins undergo severe conformational changes that lead directly to disease pathology.
* **Normal Structure:** Healthy SOD1 folds into a highly stable, eight-stranded anti-parallel beta-barrel (a "Greek key" structural motif). 
* **Mutant Instability:** ALS-associated genetic mutations disrupt the protein's native folding. They frequently impair the protein's ability to hold onto its zinc ion or form its crucial intrasubunit disulfide bond.
* **Misfolding and Aggregation:** This structural destabilization exposes sticky, hydrophobic regions of the protein that are normally hidden. The misfolded SOD1 monomers inappropriately bind to one another, forming insoluble oligomers and large protein aggregates.

## 4. Functional Level: Toxic Gain-of-Function
The functional breakdown of SOD1 creates a lethal cellular environment specifically targeting motor neurons.
* **Normal Function:** Healthy SOD1 acts as an essential cellular antioxidant. It scavenges highly reactive superoxide radicals (O₂⁻)—toxic byproducts of mitochondrial energy production—and neutralizes them into ordinary molecular oxygen (O₂) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂).
* **The ALS1 Mechanism:** While some loss of this antioxidant function occurs, ALS1 is driven primarily by a **toxic gain-of-function**. The disease is not caused by the absence of normal SOD1, but by the new, harmful properties of the mutated version.
* **Motor Neuron Death:** The structurally misfolded SOD1 aggregates physically accumulate in the cytoplasm of motor neurons. These aggregates choke the cell's internal machinery: they disrupt axonal transport, impair mitochondrial function, and induce severe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. This overwhelming cellular toxicity eventually triggers apoptosis (programmed cell death), severing the communication network between the brain and muscles and resulting in fatal paralysis.
